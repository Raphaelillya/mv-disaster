01 — Overview

What is Guyana's MV Barima tragedy?

A brief account of what happened, and why months later it remains a national reckoning.

On the night of Saturday, July 18, 2026, the MV Barima — a state-owned ferry operated by Guyana's Transport and Harbours Department — capsized and sank in the Atlantic Ocean off the Essequibo coast while carrying passengers and cargo from Georgetown to the remote North West District settlement of Port Kaituma. Officials have called it the deadliest maritime disaster in the country's history.

The ferry's departure manifest listed 133 people — 116 passengers and 17 crew — but that figure was revised upward after officials reviewed the boarding process: roughly 179 people are now understood to have been aboard. By early August, 73 bodies had been recovered and 76 people had been rescued; about 30 remain unaccounted for. The ship itself is still on the seabed. No salvage contractor has been confirmed, and families have organized under the banner "Float the Barima" to press for it to be raised.

In the months since, the tragedy has widened into a national reckoning: over the age and maintenance of state vessels, passenger registration and cargo-loading practices, the treatment and compensation of survivors and bereaved families, the independence of the Commission of Inquiry investigating the sinking, and the accountability of the officials responsible for maritime oversight. The government has charged three crew members with murder — charges their lawyers are now seeking to have stayed — opened a Commission of Inquiry, and faced sustained protest from civil society, Indigenous organizations, families, and the political opposition.

03 — Vessel & Voyage

MV Barima history & the fateful journey of July 18–19

An aging lifeline for hinterland communities, and the night it went down.

A 40-metre link to the North West

Built in Scotland in 1939, the MV Barima was 87 years old at the time of the disaster — 40.26 metres long and 8.87 metres wide, with two decks, a rated capacity of 394 passengers and 18 crew, and a cruising speed of roughly 8.5 knots. For more than four decades it had run the Georgetown–Port Kaituma route via Mabaruma and Morawhanna, carrying passengers and cargo to communities that are largely Indigenous and riverain, with few affordable alternatives to the ferry. The vessel was also uninsured at the time of the sinking. Its safety equipment was reported to include 250 life jackets and eight life rafts.

A replacement vessel already existed. In April 2023, an India-built ferry, the MV Ma Lisha, was commissioned at a cost of roughly US$12.7 million, explicitly to replace the Barima and cut the crossing time nearly in half. More than three years later, the Ma Lisha had still not entered service — officials said it was awaiting completion of a new wharf, or stelling, at Port Kaituma, originally due in 2024 and still unfinished at the time of the sinking.

04 — Response

Search, rescue & the stalled salvage

Guyana's largest-ever maritime search, hampered from the first hour — and a wreck that, months later, still hasn't been raised.

The initial rescue effort was hindered by nighttime conditions and a shortage of vessels equipped with scanning technology, leaving rescuers to rely on sight and sound to locate survivors in open water. Companies from Guyana's oil and gas sector subsequently deployed scanner-equipped vessels to assist, while the government activated divers to search the wreck for victims. The operation drew in the Guyana Defence Force Coast Guard and Air Corps, the Guyana Police Force, MARAD, the Civil Defence Commission, local divers, volunteers and private boat owners, alongside international partners including specialist divers from France, Trinidad and Tobago, and Brazil.

Formal search and recovery operations were declared concluded in early August. What hasn't happened since is the recovery of the ship itself.

The ship has not been raised

The government began looking for a salvage contractor in August. About ten companies expressed interest and three put in bids. The tender requires the ship to be raised intact, and Kaieteur News has questioned whether any of the three bidders has done that kind of salvage work before. On September 26, President Irfaan Ali said the bids were being re-evaluated; no contractor has been announced. Families have picketed under the call "Float the Barima" — they want the ship raised both to recover their loved ones' remains and to preserve the evidence the Commission of Inquiry needs.

05 — Testimony

Eyewitness accounts of survivors & others

First-hand accounts, paraphrased from public interviews and statements to Guyanese media and the Department of Public Information.

Leon Murray — Mabaruma, lost six relatives

Murray boarded with family members and was among the few to survive; he lost six relatives in the sinking. He turned down a government settlement offer of $5 million total for all six, and has told reporters he is "looking for justice" rather than a payout.

Ron Bovell — survivor, lost six family members

Bovell says the engine stalled repeatedly on the journey and that visible overloading was reported to the captain before the vessel capsized — an account that conflicts with early official statements dismissing mechanical failure and overbooking.

06 — Aftermath

Families say they have been left on their own

A support program announced early has not, by families' own accounts, reached most of them.

President Ali set up the H.E.A.R.T. Initiative to give families long-term help — legal assistance with documents, counselling, medical care, spiritual support, and help for affected children. Families say little of it has reached them. One family received two food hampers. Others say social workers stopped visiting, especially after families began to protest.

Settlement offers have also drawn criticism. Leon Murray turned down a government offer of $5 million total for the six relatives he lost. The family of chief engineer Paul Seepersad has likewise refused a proposed settlement, saying they would effectively have to "sign away their rights." Families have told reporters they were pressed to sign documents they fear would release the government from legal or financial responsibility for the sinking. Burial-cost payments have also been inconsistent: the Cort family says it received $650,000 for each missing relative, while other families received $350,000, with no explanation for the difference given.

Diane Cort · whose husband Neil is still missing

"For about three weeks now, nobody hasn't come back to my home."

Before you sign anything: families have reported being asked to sign papers in exchange for payment. You do not have to sign on the spot — ask for a copy, take your time, and have a lawyer or someone you trust read it first.
07 — Inquiry

Commission of Inquiry

An international panel with a broad mandate — now entangled with the criminal case against three crew members.

On July 26, 2026, President Irfaan Ali announced a five-member international Commission of Inquiry (COI) to investigate every phase of the disaster, chaired by retired Belizean judge Godfrey P. Smith, CARICOM's High-Level Representative for Law and Criminal Justice. Commissioners were sworn in on July 30. According to its terms of reference, the Commission examines the vessel's seaworthiness and maintenance history, passenger-capacity enforcement and manifest accuracy, cargo-loading practices, the adequacy of emergency response and search-and-rescue capacity, and regulatory oversight by MARAD — and will recommend reforms.

On September 1, the Commission formally called for evidence from the public. Its rules were gazetted on September 29, which allow survivors and bereaved relatives to apply for "standing" — a formal part in the hearings — and provide for public hearings at the Arthur Chung Conference Centre, though no hearing dates have yet been announced.

The criminal case now overlaps with the inquiry

Captain Kevin Price, Chief Mate Rondell Dwayne Roberts and Goods Superintendent Delon Granderson were charged with murder and remanded on July 28. On September 29, their lawyers asked the Charity Magistrate's Court to stay the case as an abuse of process, arguing the charges were brought before the marine casualty investigation required by Part XVIII of Guyana's Shipping Act, and that the inquiry and the criminal case now improperly overlap. The prosecution has until October 12 to respond; the magistrate is due to rule on October 19. Paper committal proceedings are separately scheduled for November 16.

Status, as of early October 2026: the Commission is actively gathering evidence, but no public hearing date has been set, the wreck has not been raised to support its work, and the parallel criminal case is itself now contested in court.
08 — Civil Society

Joint statement & petition on the COI and related issues

What a broad coalition of Guyanese civil society is formally demanding.

On September 3, a delegation of eight organizations delivered a joint civil society petition to President Ali, backed by 165 organizations and individuals across Guyana, the Caribbean and the diaspora. The statement, begun by Accountability Now Guyana, calls the sinking "wholly preventable" and asks for a Commission of Inquiry widened to include survivors, families, Indigenous leaders, civil society and the opposition — one that would:

  1. Hold its hearings in public, with interpreters for Creolese and Indigenous languages.
  2. Give the independent press full access.
  3. Appoint an independent, impartial Secretary.
  4. Record what survivors and witnesses saw, on board and during the rescue.
  5. Examine how the emergency response was coordinated.
  6. Report on the counselling given to survivors and families.
  7. Report on burial arrangements and whether families' wishes were respected.
  8. Publish all its findings and recommendations.

The statement also calls for:

  • Resignation of five officials: Ministers Juan Edghill and Deodat Indar, MARAD Director General Stephen Thomas, Transport and Harbours General Manager Yolanda Hughes-Smith, and Transport and Harbours Board Chair Rosalinda Rasul.
  • Free legal representation for survivors and bereaved families.
  • Real consultation with Indigenous and riverain communities, and sanctions for anyone who obstructs the inquiry.
  • A formal technical investigation under the International Maritime Organization's Casualty Investigation Code, its ferry safety rules, SOLAS, and Part XVIII of Guyana's Shipping Act.
  • Withdrawal of the murder charges against the captain, first mate and goods loading supervisor, so a full investigation and coroner's inquest can take place instead.
  • An end to attempts to criminalize people and groups for speaking out, assembling and organizing.

The delegation that delivered the petition came from the Amerindian Peoples Association, the Guyana Trades Union Congress, the Guyanese Organization of Indigenous Peoples, the Oil and Gas Governance Network, the Organization for the Victory of the People, Red Thread, Transparency International Guyana, and We The People.

09 — The Street

People's protests, solidarity & demands for accountability

Grief has moved from Parliament to the pavement, in more than one direction — and it hasn't stopped.

The Amerindian Peoples Association, joined by Indigenous leaders and other civil society activists, staged repeated peaceful pickets outside the Office of the President in the weeks after the sinking, calling for the resignation of Ministers Edghill and Indar, systemic reform of river transport serving hinterland communities, and salvage of the wreck. On September 15, families picketed again under the "Float the Barima" banner. Inside Parliament, the combined opposition has pressed the same demands; on September 25, Minister Edghill, asked whether he had given evidence to the Commission and whether he accepted responsibility, said only that he was "not a part of the Commission of Inquiry" and would "speak at the right time."

On September 30, relatives of those killed and missing gathered on Main Street in Georgetown as President Ali's motorcade passed, calling for the ship to be raised, the remains of the missing recovered, and Ministers Edghill and Indar to resign. The convoy did not stop.

A separate strand of protest continues in support of the three Transport and Harbours Department employees charged with murder. Family members, friends and residents have held solidarity demonstrations arguing the criminal charges scapegoat lower-level crew for failures of oversight that sit higher up the chain of command — an argument that gained a legal dimension in late September, when the accuseds' own lawyers moved to stay the case on similar grounds.

Minister Juan Edghill · 25 September

"At the right time I will speak on the matter."

10 — Legal Framework

IMO conventions & Guyana's Shipping Act

The international and domestic rules both the inquiry and the criminal case are now being measured against.

International framework

Civil society's joint statement calls for a formal technical investigation conducted under the International Maritime Organization's Casualty Investigation Code, its ferry safety rules, and the International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS) — the global framework the IMO sets for investigating marine casualties — arguing this is what would give any inquiry genuine international credibility, rather than one run entirely on domestic terms.

Guyana's Shipping Act, 1998

Domestically, maritime safety is governed by the Guyana Shipping Act (Act No. 7 of 1998), a 456-section law that also created MARAD, the agency responsible for regulating, licensing and inspecting vessels. The Act is organized into 21 parts, several of which bear directly on the questions now before both the Commission and the courts:

Part VIManning of ships & crew certification
Part XSafety of life at sea
Part XILoad lines
Part XIICarriage of bulk cargo & dangerous goods
Part XIIISeaworthiness of ships
Part XIVWreck & salvage
Part XVPassenger ships
Part XVIIIInquiries into marine casualties

Part XVIII is now at the center of an active legal dispute. On September 29, lawyers for Captain Price, Chief Mate Roberts and Goods Superintendent Granderson argued in the Charity Magistrate's Court that the murder charges against their clients were brought before the marine casualty investigation this Part requires, and asked that the case be stayed as an abuse of process. The prosecution has until October 12 to respond, with a ruling due October 19.
