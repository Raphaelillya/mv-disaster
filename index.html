What happened

The MV Barima left Georgetown at 3:15pm on Saturday 18 July, bound for Port Kaituma. At about 11pm, off the coast near the Pomeroon River, it capsized and sank.

The passenger manifest listed 133 people. Authorities later found that about 179 were on board. Survivors have described overcrowding, cargo that was not secured, engine trouble and a slow rescue.

The ferry was more than 80 years old and uninsured. A newer ferry, the MV Ma Lisha, was meant to replace it, but the new stelling at Port Kaituma it needs was due in 2024 and is still unfinished. The captain and two crew members have been charged. A Commission of Inquiry is now gathering evidence, and civil society groups are asking for it to be widened and held in public.

Where things stand now

What families, reporters and officials have said through September. In short:

  • The ship is still on the seabed and no salvage contractor has been chosen.
  • Families say promised support hasn't reached them, and some are refusing settlements they fear sign away their rights.
  • Ministers have not answered calls to resign, and families have taken to the streets.
  • The three crew members charged with murder have asked the court to stop the case.
  • Indigenous leaders are calling for the same safety standards as the rest of Guyana.

The ship has not been raised

The government began looking for a salvage contractor in August. About ten companies expressed interest and three put in bids. The tender requires the ship to be raised intact, and Kaieteur News has questioned whether any of the three has done that kind of salvage before. On 26 September President Ali said the bids are being re-evaluated. No contractor has been announced. Families have picketed under the call “Float the Barima”: they want the ship raised to recover their loved ones' remains and the evidence the inquiry needs.

“We need answers; our brother's remains.”

Gailann Thomas, who lost her brothers Kyle and Brian

“We are not hiding from salvaging; there is a process and that process is ongoing.”

President Irfaan Ali, 26 September

Families say they have been left on their own

President Ali set up the H.E.A.R.T. Initiative to give families long-term help: legal help with documents, counselling, medical care, spiritual support, and help for affected children. Families say little of it has reached them. One family received two food hampers. Others say social workers stopped visiting, especially after families began to protest.

Leon Murray of Mabaruma lost six relatives. He turned down a government offer of $5 million in total for all six. The family of chief engineer Paul Seepersad has also refused a settlement, saying they would have to “sign away their rights”. Families have told reporters they were pressed to sign documents that they fear would release the government from legal or financial responsibility for the sinking.

Payments for burial costs have not been equal either. The Cort family says it was given $650,000 for each missing relative while other families received $350,000, with no explanation for the difference.

“For about three weeks now, nobody hasn't come back to my home.”

Diane Cort, whose husband Neil is still missing

“I am looking for justice.”

Leon Murray, Mabaruma

Kiskadee Watch: 23 Sep, 24 Sep, 29 Sep. Kaieteur News: 16 Sep, 30 Sep

Ministers have not answered families

Families have called every week for the Minister of Public Works, Juan Edghill, to resign. Asked at Parliament on 25 September whether he had given evidence to the Commission and whether he accepted responsibility, he said he was “not a part of the Commission of Inquiry” and repeated that he would speak at the right time.

On 30 September, relatives of missing crew members gathered on Main Street in Georgetown as the President's motorcade passed. They called for the ship to be raised, for the remains of the missing to be recovered, and for Ministers Deodat Indar and Juan Edghill to resign. The convoy did not stop. Kaieteur News also reports that internal documents show the vessel had known technical faults before it left.

“At the right time I will speak on the matter.”

Minister Juan Edghill, 25 September

Kaieteur News: 27 Sep, 30 Sep

The accused ask the court to stop the murder case

Captain Kevin Price, chief mate Rondell Dwayne Roberts and goods superintendent Delon Granderson are charged with murder. On 29 September their lawyers asked the Charity Magistrate's Court to stay the case as an abuse of process. They argue the charges were brought before the marine casualty investigation that Part XVIII of the Shipping Act requires, and that the inquiry and the criminal case now overlap. The prosecution has until 12 October to respond. The magistrate is due to rule on 19 October.

Kiskadee Watch, 29 Sep; Kaieteur News, 29 Sep

Indigenous communities call for equal safety

Many of those aboard were from Indigenous and riverain communities in the North West. The Amerindian Peoples Association says these communities deserve the same safety standards and public services as everyone else. It is calling for an independent technical investigation to international standards, public hearings people can attend and follow, support for affected families, and publication of the inquiry's findings.

“Remember we must, forget we will not.”

Raphael Joseph, Toshao of Kangaruma

Kiskadee Watch, 10 Sep

What families and civil society are asking for

On 3 September a delegation of eight organisations delivered a joint civil society petition to President Ali, backed by 165 organisations and individuals in Guyana, the Caribbean and the diaspora. They call the sinking “wholly preventable” and ask for a Commission of Inquiry widened to include survivors, families, Indigenous leaders, civil society and the opposition, which will:

  1. Hold its hearings in public, with interpreters for Creolese and Indigenous languages.
  2. Give the independent press full access.
  3. Appoint an independent, impartial Secretary.
  4. Record what survivors and witnesses saw, on board and during the rescue.
  5. Examine how the emergency response was coordinated.
  6. Report on the counselling given to survivors and families.
  7. Report on burial arrangements and whether families' wishes were respected.
  8. Publish all its findings and recommendations.

The statement also calls for:

  • the resignation of five officials: Ministers Juan Edghill and Deodat Indar, MARAD Director General Stephen Thomas, Transport and Harbours General Manager Yolanda Hughes-Smith, and Transport and Harbours Board Chair Rosalinda Rasul
  • free legal representation for survivors and bereaved families
  • real consultation with Indigenous and riverain communities, and sanctions for anyone who obstructs the inquiry
  • a formal technical investigation under the International Maritime Organization's Casualty Investigation Code, its ferry safety rules, SOLAS and Part XVIII of Guyana's Shipping Act
  • the murder charges against the captain, first mate and goods loading supervisor to be withdrawn so a full investigation and coroner's inquest can take place
  • an end to attempts to criminalise people and groups for speaking out, assembling and organising

The statement was started by Accountability Now Guyana. The delegation that delivered it came from the Amerindian Peoples Association, the Guyana Trades Union Congress, the Guyanese Organization of Indigenous Peoples, the Oil and Gas Governance Network, the Organization for the Victory of the People, Red Thread, Transparency International Guyana and We The People.

If you lost someone, ask for a place at the Inquiry

Survivors and relatives of those who died can apply for “standing”, which gives them a formal part in the hearings. Write to the Commission's Secretary with your contact details and your lawyer's, explain your request in no more than three pages, and include any documents that support it. There is no closing date yet. You can have a lawyer with you when you give evidence.

Hearings will be at the Arthur Chung Conference Centre, Monday to Friday, 9am to 4pm, and open to the public and press unless the Commission decides otherwise. Dates have not been announced.

Before you sign anything

Some families say they have been asked to sign papers in return for payment. You do not have to sign on the spot. Ask for a copy, take your time, and have a lawyer or someone you trust read it first.
